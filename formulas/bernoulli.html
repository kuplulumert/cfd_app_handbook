Conservation Law

Bernoulli Equation

Energy is conserved along a streamline in ideal fluid flow

\frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{v^2}{2g} + z = \text{constant}

Valid for: Steady, incompressible, inviscid flow along streamlines

What Each Symbol Means

p

Pressure

Pascals (Pa)

Static pressure - the normal stress exerted by fluid on surfaces

Example: 101,325 Pa (atmospheric), 200,000 Pa (typical pump discharge)

ρ

Density

kg/m³

Mass per unit volume - determines fluid's inertial properties

Example: 998 kg/m³ (water), 1.225 kg/m³ (air at STP)

g

Gravity

m/s²

Gravitational acceleration - creates hydrostatic pressure gradients

Example: 9.81 m/s² (standard), varies ±0.5% globally

v

Velocity

m/s

Local fluid velocity magnitude - creates dynamic pressure effects

Example: 2 m/s (pipe flow), 50 m/s (aircraft), 300 m/s (turbine)

z

Elevation

m

Height above datum - stores gravitational potential energy

Example: 50 m (water tower), 10 km (aircraft altitude)

🏗️ Think Like a Hydraulic Engineer

Bernoulli's equation is like designing a hydraulic system where you manage three forms of mechanical energy. Each term represents a different way energy is stored and transported in the flowing fluid.

⚡

Pressure Head (p/ρg)

Like a compressed air tank - stored energy ready to do work. High pressure means high potential to accelerate fluid or lift it to higher elevations. Measured in meters of fluid column.

🌊

Velocity Head (v²/2g)

Like a moving turbine - kinetic energy in motion. Fast-moving fluid has energy tied up in its momentum. Can be converted to pressure by slowing the flow down.

🏔️

Elevation Head (z)

Like a water tower - gravitational potential energy. Higher elevation stores energy that can drive flow downward. Every 10 meters = ~1 bar of pressure equivalent.

🔧 Engineering Insight: Total mechanical energy per unit weight stays constant along a streamline. Energy can transform between forms but cannot be created or destroyed (absent friction or work input).

Engineering Example: Reservoir Discharge

🚰 Torricelli's Law: Free Jet from Reservoir

📋 Given Conditions

  • • Large reservoir (v₁ ≈ 0)
  • • Height difference: h = 5 m
  • • Both points at atmospheric pressure
  • • Sharp-edged orifice (assume inviscid)
  • • Find: Discharge velocity

⚖️ Energy Balance

Apply Bernoulli between reservoir surface and jet exit. Since p₁ = p₂ and v₁ ≈ 0, all elevation head converts to velocity head:

v₂ = √(2gh) = 9.9 m/s

Physical meaning: Same velocity as free-fall from height h. This is why fire hoses and water jets work!

When Bernoulli Breaks Down

Bernoulli assumes inviscid, steady, incompressible flow. Real engineering situations where it fails:

🌀 Viscous Boundary Layers

Near walls where no-slip condition creates velocity gradients. Viscous stresses violate the inviscid assumption. Use boundary layer theory instead.

💨 Compressible Flow (Ma > 0.3)

When fluid density changes significantly with pressure. Gas flows in turbomachinery, nozzles, or high-speed applications need compressible flow equations.

⚙️ Turbomachinery

Across pumps, compressors, turbines where work is added or extracted. Need to include shaft work terms in energy equation.

📊 Unsteady Acceleration

Rapidly changing flow conditions like water hammer or pulsating flow. Unsteady terms in momentum equation become significant.

🔥 Heat Transfer Effects

When temperature changes affect density or when thermal energy is significant compared to mechanical energy.

🌪️ Flow Separation

Behind bluff bodies or in adverse pressure gradients where streamlines break down and recirculation occurs.
