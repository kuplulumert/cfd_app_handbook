SIMPLE

Semi-Implicit Method for Pressure-Linked Equations

1. Method Overview

  • Segregated pressure-velocity coupling algorithm for incompressible flows using predictor-corrector approach.
  • Industry standard implemented in every major commercial CFD solver with proven reliability.
  • Sequential solution approach solving momentum and pressure correction equations separately.
  • Developed by Patankar and Spalding (1972) as the foundational CFD algorithm for engineering applications.

2. Key Strengths

  • Computational efficiency: Low memory requirements, 40-60% less memory than coupled solvers.
  • Robust convergence: Stable and predictable across wide range of flow conditions.
  • Universal availability: Standard implementation in all commercial CFD packages.
  • Extensive validation: 50+ years of industrial validation across diverse applications.
  • Simple implementation: Straightforward coding and easy to understand algorithm structure.

3. Limitations

  • Convergence speed: Slower than coupled methods for complex geometries and strong coupling.
  • Under-relaxation dependency: Requires careful tuning of relaxation factors for stability.
  • Transient limitations: Poor performance for highly unsteady flows compared to PISO.
  • Strong coupling issues: Struggles with high Rayleigh number natural convection flows.

4. Ideal Applications

Steady Internal Flows: Pipe networks, ducts, heat exchangers with established flow patterns
HVAC Systems: Room air distribution, ventilation design, thermal comfort analysis
Preliminary Design: Early-stage analysis where computational speed is prioritized
Large Domains: External flows around buildings, urban environments
Simple Geometries: Straight channels, pipes without complex flow separation
Educational Use: Teaching fundamental CFD concepts and pressure-velocity coupling

5. When to Choose SIMPLE Over Other Methods

  • Computational budget constraints: When memory and speed are more important than convergence rate.
  • Steady-state simulations: Primary application domain with minimal transient effects.
  • Simple geometries: Straight ducts, pipes, and channels without complex flow features.
  • Engineering accuracy: When 1-5% accuracy is sufficient for design decisions.
  • Legacy compatibility: When consistency with historical simulation data is required.

6. When NOT to Use SIMPLE

Highly transient flows: Unsteady mixing, oscillating flows, start-up processes
Strong pressure-velocity coupling: Natural convection with high Rayleigh numbers
Complex multiphase: VOF simulations with high density ratios
Research accuracy needs: When precision < 0.1% error is required
Poor mesh quality: High skewness, aspect ratios > 100, non-orthogonal meshes

7. Setup and Guidelines

Solver Settings:

  • Under-relaxation factors: Pressure 0.3, Momentum 0.7, conservative starting values.
  • Discretization: Second-order upwind for momentum, PRESTO! for pressure.
  • Convergence: Residuals < 10⁻⁴ typically adequate for engineering accuracy.
  • Initialization: Use potential flow or previous solution for better starting point.

Mesh Requirements:

  • Aspect ratio: Keep < 100 in core flow regions for optimal convergence.
  • Skewness: Maintain < 0.95 for stable pressure correction equation.
  • Growth ratio: < 1.3 preferred, especially in pressure gradient regions.
  • Domain size: Ensure adequate outlet distance for pressure boundary conditions.

Boundary Conditions:

  • Inlet conditions: Specify velocity or mass flow rate with appropriate profiles.
  • Outlet conditions: Use pressure outlet for better pressure-velocity coupling.
  • Wall treatment: No-slip conditions with appropriate wall functions if needed.
  • Symmetry planes: Use for computational efficiency in symmetric geometries.

Historical Context and Development

The SIMPLE algorithm represents one of the most fundamental breakthroughs in computational fluid dynamics, developed by Suhas Patankar and Brian Spalding at Imperial College London in 1972. This algorithm solved the critical challenge of pressure-velocity coupling in incompressible flow, where the continuity equation provides a constraint on velocity but no direct equation for pressure.

Timeline and Evolution

The pressure-velocity coupling problem had plagued early CFD development since the 1960s. Attempts using primitive variables (u, v, w, p) faced numerical instabilities due to the lack of a direct pressure equation. SIMPLE introduced a systematic predictor-corrector approach that enabled stable, convergent solutions for incompressible flows.

Following the original SIMPLE formulation, numerous variants emerged including SIMPLER (1980) by Patankar, SIMPLEC (1984) by Van Doormaal and Raithby, and PISO (1986) by Issa. Each addressed specific limitations while maintaining the core segregated philosophy that made SIMPLE computationally efficient.

Mathematical Foundation and Complete Derivation

The SIMPLE algorithm employs a segregated approach, solving the momentum and continuity equations sequentially through a predictor-corrector methodology that ensures mass conservation while maintaining computational efficiency.

Step 1: Momentum Predictor:

$$\frac{\partial \rho \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u} \mathbf{u}) = -\nabla p^* + \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau} + \rho \mathbf{g}$$

Solve momentum equations using guessed pressure field p* to obtain predicted velocity u*

Step 2: Pressure Correction Equation:

$$\nabla \cdot \left( \frac{1}{a_p} \nabla p' \right) = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}^*$$

Derive and solve pressure correction equation to enforce mass conservation

Step 3: Field Updates:

$$p^{n+1} = p^* + \alpha_p p'$$\n$$\mathbf{u}^{n+1} = \mathbf{u}^* - \frac{1}{a_p}\nabla p'$$

Update pressure and velocity fields using correction with under-relaxation
