Inviscid Flow

Ideal flow model - Perfect for high-speed flows, conceptual design, and far-field analysis

✅
Use Inviscid When (Industry Focus)
Situation Why It Works Advice
High-speed flows (Ma > 0.3, supersonic) Inertial forces dominate over viscous effects Perfect for shock wave analysis and supersonic design
Conceptual design phase (preliminary analysis) Fast results for design space exploration Use before detailed viscous analysis
Far-field flow analysis (away from walls) Viscous effects negligible far from boundaries Good for outer flow field predictions
Potential flow validation (CFD benchmarking) Analytical solutions available for comparison Excellent for code verification
Educational purposes (flow physics understanding) Simple, fundamental flow physics Great for learning basic CFD principles
⚠️
Avoid Inviscid When (Industry Focus)
Situation Why It Fails Industry Advice
Any internal flows (pipes, ducts, HVAC) Wall friction dominates, no viscous forces Use laminar or turbulent models
Drag force predictions (any application) Cannot predict viscous drag (skin friction) Always use viscous models for drag
Heat transfer analysis (any thermal problem) No thermal boundary layer, no wall heat transfer Must include viscous effects for heat transfer
Low-speed flows (Ma < 0.1, most industrial) Viscous effects significant at low speeds Use appropriate viscous model
Boundary layer analysis (near-wall flows) Cannot capture viscous boundary layer physics Essential to include viscosity near walls
Final design validation (production CFD) Too idealized for real-world accuracy Only for conceptual phase, not final validation
🔑
Industry Takeaway

✅ Conceptual Design

Perfect for preliminary analysis and design space exploration - fast results to guide direction.

⚡ High-Speed Only

Most useful for high-speed flows (Ma > 0.3) where inertial forces dominate viscous effects.

🎯 Never Final

Always follow up with viscous analysis for real-world applications - inviscid is just the first step.

Governing Equations
Continuity Equation
$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u}) = 0$$

Mass conservation - no source/sink terms

Momentum Conservation
$$\frac{\partial (\rho \mathbf{u})}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u} \mathbf{u}) = -\nabla p + \rho \mathbf{g}$$

No viscous terms - only pressure and body forces

Energy Conservation
$$\frac{\partial (\rho E)}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot [(\rho E + p) \mathbf{u}] = \rho \mathbf{g} \cdot \mathbf{u}$$

No viscous dissipation or heat conduction

Equation of State
$$p = \rho R T \quad \text{or} \quad p = (\gamma - 1) \rho e$$

Closes the system - relates pressure, density, and temperature

Key Assumptions
μ = 0
No viscosity
k = 0
No heat conduction
Slip BC
Flow slips along walls
High Re
Inertial >> Viscous forces
No Separation
Cannot predict flow separation
Pressure Only
Only pressure drag, no skin friction

Engineering Analogy

🏎️ Frictionless Racing Track

Inviscid flow is like a frictionless racing track:

  • No friction: "Cars can slide perfectly along the track surface"
  • Only inertia matters: "Speed and direction changes come from track shape"
  • Perfect for high speeds: "At high speeds, friction becomes less important"
  • Conceptual understanding: "Great for understanding basic racing lines and forces"
  • Not realistic: "Real racing needs friction for control and stopping"
