Laminar Flow Model

Viscous flow model based on Navier-Stokes equations for smooth, ordered fluid motion

🎯 CFD Guidance - Laminar Flow

✅ When to Use Laminar Flow (Critical Applications)

  • Low Reynolds number flows (Re < 2,300 for pipes, Re < 5×10⁵ for external) - Viscous forces dominate
  • Microfluidics and MEMS - Very small scale flows where Re << 1
  • High viscosity fluids - Oils, polymers, biological fluids
  • Creeping flows - Stokes flow regime (Re << 1)
  • Heat transfer in smooth channels - Predictable thermal boundary layers
  • Transition region analysis - Before turbulence onset
  • Benchmark validation - Exact solutions available (Poiseuille, Couette, Stokes)
  • Precise drag calculations - Accurate viscous drag prediction

⚠️ When to Avoid Laminar Flow (Critical Limitations)

  • High Reynolds number flows (Re > 4,000 for pipes) - Flow will be turbulent
  • Industrial-scale applications - Most practical flows are turbulent
  • External flows over bodies at high Re - Boundary layer will transition
  • Mixing applications - Turbulence enhances mixing significantly
  • Heat transfer enhancement - Turbulence improves heat transfer rates
  • Rough surfaces - Surface roughness triggers transition to turbulence
  • High-speed flows - Compressibility and instabilities promote turbulence
  • Large computational domains - Expensive for high Re flows

Setup Tips & Best Practices

  • No-slip boundary conditions - Velocity = 0 at all solid walls
  • Adequate grid resolution - Resolve viscous boundary layers completely
  • Steady vs unsteady - Use steady for Re < critical, unsteady near transition
  • Monitor flow stability - Check for transition to turbulence
  • Validate against theory - Compare with analytical solutions when available
  • Convergence criteria - Tight residuals (< 1e-6) for viscous accuracy

📐 Navier-Stokes Equations - Detailed Analysis

1️⃣ Continuity Equation (Mass Conservation)

\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u}) = 0

Physical Meaning: Mass conservation - same as inviscid flow

∂ρ/∂t: Rate of density change at fixed point

∇·(ρu): Net mass flux divergence

Incompressible form: ∇·u = 0 (when ρ = constant)

2️⃣ Momentum Equation (Newton's 2nd Law + Viscosity)

\frac{\partial (\rho \mathbf{u})}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u} \otimes \mathbf{u}) + \nabla p = \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau} + \rho \mathbf{f}

Key Difference from Inviscid: Added viscous stress tensor ∇·τ

∂(ρu)/∂t: Local acceleration (momentum change rate)

∇·(ρu⊗u): Convective acceleration = ρu·∇u

∇p: Pressure gradient force

∇·τ: Viscous stress divergence = μ∇²u + (μ+λ)∇(∇·u) for Newtonian fluids

ρf: Body forces (gravity, etc.)

For incompressible Newtonian: ρ(∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = -∇p + μ∇²u + ρf

3️⃣ Energy Equation (1st Law + Heat Conduction)

\frac{\partial E}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot ((E + p) \mathbf{u}) = \nabla \cdot (k \nabla T) + \boldsymbol{\tau} : \nabla \mathbf{u} + \rho \mathbf{f} \cdot \mathbf{u}

Key Additions from Inviscid: Heat conduction + viscous dissipation

∇·(k∇T): Heat conduction where k = thermal conductivity

τ:∇u: Viscous dissipation = τᵢⱼ(∂uᵢ/∂xⱼ) (always positive, irreversible)

Temperature form: ρcₚ(∂T/∂t + u·∇T) = k∇²T + Φ for constant properties

Φ: Viscous dissipation function = μ[(∂uᵢ/∂xⱼ + ∂uⱼ/∂xᵢ)]²

4️⃣ Viscous Stress Tensor

\tau_{ij} = \mu \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) + \lambda \delta_{ij} \frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k}

Physical Meaning: Stress due to velocity gradients (deformation)

μ: Dynamic viscosity (shear viscosity)

λ: Second viscosity (bulk viscosity, often ≈ 0)

∂uᵢ/∂xⱼ + ∂uⱼ/∂xᵢ: Symmetric strain rate tensor

δᵢⱼ∇·u: Volumetric strain contribution

Newtonian assumption: Linear relationship between stress and strain rate

💡 Viscous Flow Analogy

Think of laminar flow as honey flowing smoothly in layers. Each layer slides past its neighbors in an orderly fashion, with friction between layers causing energy dissipation.

Viscous stress: Like friction between sliding layers - faster layers drag slower ones forward, slower layers hold back faster ones.

No-slip condition: Fluid "sticks" to solid walls - like honey clinging to a spoon surface.

Boundary layers: Velocity changes from zero at wall to free-stream value - like the transition zone when honey meets air.

Energy dissipation: Viscous friction converts kinetic energy to heat - why stirring honey warms it up.

🌊 Flow Regimes & Transitions

Flow Type Reynolds Number Characteristics Examples
Creeping Flow Re << 1 Viscous forces dominate, no inertia Microfluidics, blood flow
Laminar Pipe Re < 2,300 Smooth, predictable, parabolic profile Oil pipelines, IV tubes
Laminar External Re < 5×10⁵ Smooth boundary layer, no separation Airfoil leading edge, small objects
Transition Zone 2,300 < Re < 4,000 Intermittent turbulence, unstable Pipe entrance, flow around cylinders

📚 Classical Analytical Solutions

Poiseuille Flow (Pipe)

u(r) = \frac{\Delta p}{4\mu L}(R^2 - r^2)

Parabolic velocity profile, maximum at centerline

Couette Flow (Parallel Plates)

u(y) = U \frac{y}{h}

Linear velocity profile between moving plates

Stokes Flow (Sphere)

F_D = 6\pi \mu R U

Drag force on sphere in creeping flow (Re << 1)
