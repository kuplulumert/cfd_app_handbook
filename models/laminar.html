Laminar Flow

Viscous flow model - Perfect for low Reynolds number flows and smooth, ordered motion

✅
Use Laminar When (Industry Focus)
Situation Why It Works Advice
Low Reynolds number flows (Re < 2,300) Viscous forces dominate, flow remains smooth Perfect accuracy for low Re applications
Microfluidics (small devices, lab-on-chip) Small scales ensure laminar conditions Standard choice for microfluidic devices
Viscous fluids (oils, honey, polymers) High viscosity suppresses turbulence Use for high-viscosity industrial processes
Heat transfer accuracy critical (electronics cooling) Precise thermal boundary layer resolution Best for precise heat transfer calculations
Educational/validation purposes Analytical solutions available for comparison Excellent for CFD validation and learning
⚠️
Avoid Laminar When (Industry Focus)
Situation Why It Fails Industry Advice
High Reynolds number flows (Re > 4,000) Flow becomes turbulent, laminar assumption invalid Use appropriate turbulence model
Most industrial equipment (pumps, fans, HVAC) Operating Re typically > 10,000, fully turbulent Use k-ε or k-ω SST for industrial equipment
External aerodynamics (cars, aircraft) High-speed flows are always turbulent Use k-ω SST or Spalart-Allmaras
Transitional flow regions (mixed laminar/turbulent) Cannot predict transition to turbulence Use transition models for transition prediction
Large-scale mixing (industrial reactors) Turbulent mixing much more effective Use turbulence models for mixing applications
Unsteady flows with disturbances Disturbances can trigger transition to turbulence Consider stability analysis or turbulence models
🔑
Industry Takeaway

✅ Low Re Specialist

Use laminar for Re < 2,300 - gives perfect accuracy when flow is actually laminar.

⚡ Check Re First

Always verify Reynolds number - most industrial flows are turbulent (Re > 4,000).

🎯 Perfect When Right

When conditions are truly laminar, gives exact solution - no modeling uncertainty.

Navier-Stokes Equations
Continuity (Mass Conservation)
$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u}) = 0$$
Momentum Conservation
$$\frac{\partial (\rho \mathbf{u})}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u} \mathbf{u}) = -\nabla p + \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau} + \rho \mathbf{g}$$

Includes viscous stress tensor τ

Viscous Stress Tensor
$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mu \left[\nabla \mathbf{u} + (\nabla \mathbf{u})^T - \frac{2}{3}(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u})\mathbf{I}\right]$$

Newtonian fluid viscous stress

Engineering Analogy

🍯 Honey Flow

Laminar flow is like honey flowing smoothly:

  • Smooth layers: "Honey flows in perfectly smooth, parallel layers"
  • No mixing: "Different layers don't mix - you can see streaks"
  • Predictable: "Flow pattern is completely predictable and stable"
  • Viscosity matters: "Thickness (viscosity) controls how fast it flows"
  • Perfect for small scales: "Works beautifully in small containers or slow speeds"
