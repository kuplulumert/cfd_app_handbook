⚡ Two-Equation RANS Model

k-ε Turbulence Model

Two-equation RANS model solving transport equations for turbulent kinetic energy (k) and dissipation rate (ε)

🎯 CFD Guidance - k-ε Model

✅ When to Use k-ε Model (Critical Applications)

  • High Reynolds number flows (Re > 10,000) - Fully turbulent attached flows
  • Industrial applications with complex geometry - Heat exchangers, mixing chambers
  • External aerodynamics - Aircraft, automotive, building flows away from walls
  • Jet flows and mixing - Free shear layers, wakes, plumes
  • Computational efficiency critical - Large-scale simulations, parametric studies
  • Standard engineering analysis - Well-established, widely validated
  • Flows without strong pressure gradients - Gradually varying flows

⚠️ When to Avoid k-ε Model (Critical Limitations)

  • Near-wall regions without wall functions - Cannot handle viscous sublayer (y+ < 30)
  • Strong streamline curvature - Overpredicts turbulence in curved flows
  • Rotating or swirling flows - Missing rotation/curvature corrections
  • Low Reynolds number flows - Viscous effects dominate, damping functions needed
  • Separated flows - Poor prediction of separation and reattachment
  • Strong adverse pressure gradients - Premature separation prediction
  • Transitional flows - Cannot predict laminar-turbulent transition
  • Buoyancy-driven flows - Needs additional buoyancy corrections

Setup Tips & Best Practices

  • Wall treatment: Use y+ > 30 with wall functions, or y+ < 1 with enhanced wall treatment
  • Initialization: k = 1.5(UI)², ε = C_μ^(3/4) k^(3/2) / (0.07L) where I = turbulence intensity, L = length scale
  • Boundary conditions: Specify k and ε at inlets based on turbulence intensity and length scale
  • Convergence: Monitor residuals for both k and ε equations (< 1e-4 for engineering accuracy)
  • Realizability: Check for negative k or ε values, use realizable k-ε if needed
  • Grid requirements: Adequate resolution in high-gradient regions, especially shear layers

📐 Transport Equations - Detailed Analysis

1️⃣ Turbulent Kinetic Energy (k) Equation

\frac{\partial k}{\partial t} + U_j \frac{\partial k}{\partial x_j} = P_k - \varepsilon + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left[\left(\nu + \frac{\nu_t}{\sigma_k}\right)\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_j}\right]

Physical Meaning: Energy balance for turbulent kinetic energy

∂k/∂t: Rate of k change at fixed point (unsteady term)

U_j ∂k/∂x_j: Convection of k by mean flow

P_k: Production of k by mean shear = ν_t (∂U_i/∂x_j)(∂U_i/∂x_j + ∂U_j/∂x_i)

-ε: Dissipation of k to heat (sink term)

Diffusion term: (ν + ν_t/σ_k) ∂²k/∂x_j² where σ_k = 1.0

2️⃣ Dissipation Rate (ε) Equation

\frac{\partial \varepsilon}{\partial t} + U_j \frac{\partial \varepsilon}{\partial x_j} = C_{1\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon}{k}P_k - C_{2\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon^2}{k} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left[\left(\nu + \frac{\nu_t}{\sigma_\varepsilon}\right)\frac{\partial \varepsilon}{\partial x_j}\right]

Physical Meaning: Transport equation for dissipation rate (modeled, not exact)

∂ε/∂t: Rate of ε change at fixed point

U_j ∂ε/∂x_j: Convection of ε by mean flow

C_1ε (ε/k)P_k: Production of ε proportional to k production (C_1ε = 1.44)

-C_2ε ε²/k: Destruction of ε (C_2ε = 1.92)

Diffusion term: (ν + ν_t/σ_ε) ∂²ε/∂x_j² where σ_ε = 1.3

3️⃣ Turbulent Viscosity Closure

\nu_t = C_\mu \frac{k^2}{\varepsilon}

Physical Meaning: Eddy viscosity based on k and ε

C_μ = 0.09: Model constant from dimensional analysis

k²/ε: Velocity scale × length scale = (√k) × (k^(3/2)/ε)

Dimensional check: [ν_t] = [k²/ε] = (m²/s²)²/(m²/s³) = m²/s ✓

k

💡 Energy Cascade Analogy

Think of turbulent flow as a waterfall cascade. Large eddies (k) are like big rocks creating turbulence at the top. Energy cascades down through smaller and smaller eddies until it's finally dissipated as heat (ε) at the molecular level.

k equation: Tracks the energy in the "turbulent reservoir" - production from mean flow minus losses to dissipation.

ε equation: Controls how fast energy cascades down the eddy spectrum to molecular dissipation.
